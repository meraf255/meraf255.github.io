Storglaciären, in northern Sweden, is internationally recognized as a "glaciological lab," meaning decades of prior monitoring data exist for comparison. The glacier has an annual mean temperature of -6°C and an average ice thickness of 95m (maximum 250m as of 1996).
Investigate the loss of the glacier over time and, specifically, how to measure uncertainty in that volume-change estimate in the presence of spatial correlation in the underlying elevation data.
We used Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) built from LiDAR and photogrammetry survey data at different time points. We treated the glacier as a system (accumulation vs. ablation) and removed outliers. We co-registered elevation surfaces across time points, which reduced the normalized median absolute deviation (NMAD) from 3.4m to 2.5m. We addressed data voids using McNabb's (2019) void-filling approach combined with hypsometric interpolation: binning elevation into 50m-wide bins, fitting a third-order polynomial regression model on bin centers (weighted by observation count), and predicting missing values.

Elevation-difference map of Storglaciären. Red areas show surface lowering (thinning) of up to 15m, concentrated in the glacier's central and eastern regions.

The interpolation model fit the elevation-difference data well (multiple R-squared = 0.977, adjusted R-squared = 0.968). The resulting elevation-difference map shows the glacier has thinned most severely in its central and eastern regions, with some areas showing over 15m of surface lowering. This pipeline lays the groundwork for a subsequent, full uncertainty estimate of total volume change that explicitly models spatial correlation in the data.

Medicaid provides healthcare coverage to low-income individuals and families, and given the well-established link between poverty and poor health, it serves as a vital safety net. Prior research shows long-term challenges in access tied to income and discriminatory treatment, and utilization of care among racial and ethnic minorities has increased since the 1960s, though gaps persist.
Explore the factors that influence Medicaid beneficiaries' access to healthcare, with a focus on two research questions: how does ethnicity impact access to health services, and how does access differ based on gender and ethnicity?
Data came from the Applied Econometrics with R package (a 1986 Medicaid Consumer Survey from Santa Barbara and Ventura counties, California). We fit simple and multiple linear regression models and a logistic regression model, then used stepwise selection (AIC criterion) with k-fold cross-validation, and lasso and ridge regression to identify the most important predictors. We checked variance inflation factors (VIF) to confirm low multicollinearity.

Correlogram of access and candidate predictors. Circle size and color intensity reflect correlation strength; access shows only weak correlation with age, children, and the two principal health components.

Simple models using ethnicity alone showed no significant difference in predicted access between Caucasian and non-Caucasian respondents. The stepwise-selected model retained number of doctor visits, first and second principal health components, program enrollment, and program type as predictors (though R-squared was low, around 0.06). The lasso-selected model retained only enrollment status, with enrolled respondents having 0.41 higher predicted access. Logistic regression confirmed neither ethnicity nor gender was a statistically significant predictor of access after accounting for the other. Health status, program enrollment, and utilization were the strongest predictors of access overall, not demographic characteristics.

Conflict in refugee camps arises from scarcity of resources, land disputes, strained relationships with host communities, and internal divisions reflecting conflicts in refugees' countries of origin. This paper focuses on the South Sudanese and Ethiopian refugee crises, with Uganda hosting over 40% of the region's South Sudanese refugees and roughly 10,000 Ethiopian refugees as of early 2024.
Model how conflict levels and resolution-strategy effort interact over time in refugee camps, and compare three resolution strategies — negotiation, mediation, and arbitration — to understand which are most effective under different conflict intensities. A secondary aim is to challenge the stigma that frames refugees as sources of instability rather than active participants in peace-building.
We adapted the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model, adding a carrying capacity term (maximum sustainable conflict level) and a nonlinear term indicating that larger conflicts require disproportionately more resolution effort. Parameters (conflict growth rate, resolution effectiveness, conflict-driven effort, resource limitation) were tuned differently for each of the three strategies based on their real-world characteristics. We identified nullclines and equilibrium points, used the Jacobian matrix and its eigenvalues to assess stability, and tested three initial conditions representing low (5,5), moderate (25,10), and high (45,5) conflict levels.

Phase portrait for the negotiation strategy, showing trajectories from three initial conditions (low, moderate, high conflict) moving through the conflict/effort state space.

Negotiation drives the system toward a stable equilibrium in low- and moderate-conflict scenarios but has an unstable spiral source nearby, meaning small disturbances can temporarily escalate tension. Mediation behaves similarly, stabilizing low-conflict cases but becoming unpredictable and potentially oscillatory as conflict grows. Arbitration is largely unstable across equilibria, reflecting its resource-intensive, rigid nature — it can resolve high-stakes conflicts but risks amplifying instability if mistimed. A hybrid approach that starts with negotiation and switches to arbitration partway through outperformed arbitration alone, combining negotiation's relationship-building with arbitration's decisive resolution.

Hurricane Michael, a category 5 storm that hit Florida in 2018, destroyed tree canopy and box turtle habitat; cleanup with heavy machinery worsened the damage. The Bay County Box Turtle Project, under the US Fish and Wildlife Service, uses trained citizen scientists to tag and track turtles as an indicator of the area's ecological recovery.
Analyze the health, habitat use, and movement patterns of box turtles in Bay County to understand ecological recovery following the hurricane, specifically examining how movement behavior (stationary vs. non-stationary) and air temperature relate to turtle health.
From an initial 3,928 observations of 1,055 turtles, we restricted the dataset to recaptured/tagged turtles with complete shell-length measurements, yielding 608 observations across 441 individual turtles. We built a multilevel regression model (squared straight carapace length as the response) with turtle-level random effects to account for repeated measurements on the same animal, using years since the hurricane, air temperature, and stationarity as predictors.

Straight carapace length trajectories for five tagged turtles over time — variability within and between individual turtles motivated the multilevel modeling approach.

Years since the hurricane, air temperature, and stationarity were all significant predictors (t ≥ 1.96) of shell length. Turtles captured later were about 484.9mm² larger (squared length) per year than those captured earlier; each one-degree increase in air temperature was associated with a 52.3mm² increase; and stationary turtles were 866.08mm² smaller than turtles that were moving when captured — contrary to our initial expectation that staying put would indicate a well-adapted, thriving turtle. Turtle-to-turtle variation in shell length (618mm) was larger than variation within a single turtle across captures (101.9mm). These relationships are correlational, not causal.

Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) converts alcohols to aldehydes while reducing NAD+ to NADH, and is found across animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria. Prior research (Benner, 1995) found yeast ADH has narrower substrate specificity than other ADH variants and is most active with ethanol, with activity declining as alcohol chain length increases.
Test how increasing chain length across four straight-chain primary alcohols — ethanol, methanol, 1-propanol, and 1-octanol — affects yeast ADH's binding affinity (Km) and enzyme turnover (Vmax).
We prepared a yeast ADH stock solution buffered with TRIS pH9 and BSA, and diluted each alcohol substrate to eight concentration levels. Using a plate reader with automated enzyme injection, we measured absorbance at 340nm every 30 seconds for 20 minutes across all alcohol/concentration combinations, with NAD+ and buffer held constant. Initial reaction rates were estimated via linear regression on the earliest time points, then plotted against substrate concentration and fit to the Michaelis-Menten equation using nonlinear regression (nls) to estimate Vmax and Km for each alcohol.

Fitted Michaelis-Menten curves for methanol, propanol, and octanol, showing methanol's higher reaction rate at saturating concentrations despite its short chain length.

Ethanol showed the highest Vmax (0.000574 mol/min) and a low Km (0.060 mM), confirming strong, efficient binding. 1-Propanol and 1-Octanol reacted with ADH but at markedly lower rates, generally supporting the hypothesis that longer carbon chains hinder interaction with ADH's active site. 1-Propanol showed a notably high Km (0.443 mM), suggesting a weaker binding affinity possibly related to its molecular structure. Methanol was an exception to the overall pattern: its Vmax (0.000134 mol/min) was slightly lower than ethanol's, contradicting the expectation that the shortest chain would react fastest.

The MNIST dataset of handwritten digit images is a standard benchmark for classification methods. This project starts with a binary classification task (distinguishing 2s from 4s) and extends to a harder three-class problem (adding 6s) to see how model performance degrades with complexity.
Build and compare classification models — LDA, QDA, logistic regression, multinomial regression, and cross-validated KNN — using engineered features, and diagnose exactly where and why misclassification occurs as the number of digit classes increases.
Feature 1 was the count of empty (non-inked) pixels per image, used to fit and compare LDA and QDA models. Feature 2 was more involved: for each image, we extracted the (x,y) coordinates of ink pixels, fit a simple logistic regression of y on x, and used the resulting model's AIC value as a per-image feature — a way of capturing how "spread out" or structured the digit's shape is. This feature was used to fit logistic regression and a cross-validated KNN model (tuned over neighbor counts via 10-fold cross-validation).
get_aic <- function(num_list){
  aic_vec <- c()
  n_images <- length(num_list$images[, 1])
  for (i in 1:n_images) {
    imagei <- make_img_matrix(num_list$images[i,])
    img_df <- get_xy(imagei)
    model <- glm(vecY ~ vecX, data = img_df)
    aic_vec[i] = model$aic
  }
  return(aic_vec)
}

Rising U.S. healthcare costs, not entitlement spending, are argued to be the greatest single threat to the nation's long-term fiscal stability. Atul Gawande's "Cost Conundrum" case study of McAllen, Texas found that high local healthcare costs were driven by financial incentives for overtesting and overtreatment, not worse population health. U.S. healthcare expenditure reached $3.0 trillion in 2014.
Investigate how personal consumption expenditure on tobacco, insurance, and housing and utilities relates to U.S. healthcare spending, and build a forecasting model to understand and predict these dynamics.
Using annual U.S. data from 1959–2022 (Bureau of Economic Analysis), we deflated all series to real (inflation-adjusted) terms. We tested each series for stationarity using the Phillips-Perron test, then took first and log differences of non-stationary variables. We estimated a structural vector autoregression (VAR) model with one lag (selected via AIC/Schwarz Bayesian Criterion), identifying shocks via Cholesky factorization ordered health care → tobacco → housing/utilities → insurance. We computed impulse response functions, Granger causality tests, and forecast error variance decomposition over a 5-year horizon.

Personal health care expenditure by year (2010–2022), showing the steady upward trend and the pandemic-driven dip in 2020.

A 1% shock to tobacco expenditure produced a significant decrease in healthcare expenditure. A 1% shock to housing and utilities expenditure was associated with a decrease in insurance expenditure. Granger causality tests found significant instantaneous causality between housing/utilities and the other three variables (p=0.03) and between insurance and the other three (p=0.02), but healthcare expenditure did not Granger-cause the other variables (p=0.403). Forecast error variance decomposition showed that after four years, about 12% of the variation in healthcare expenditure was driven by tobacco shocks and about 6% by insurance shocks.

Globally, roughly one-third of food produced is lost or wasted. In catering settings, waste arises during food preparation and from consumer choices driven by taste, aesthetics, and oversized portions. College cafeterias are a significant setting for this problem, since young adults eat there frequently and self-serve, often taking more food than they can finish.
Study food waste at St. Olaf College's cafeteria (catered by Bon App) as a principal-agent problem, and evaluate the effectiveness of four policy levers: fostering communication between catering staff and students, setting a weight-based food-waste threshold, resizing plates, and running awareness campaigns.
We modeled the decision to "overplate" as a game between an individual student and the collective student body. Pre-policy, this is a prisoner's dilemma: both players' dominant strategy is to overplate, even though not overplating is jointly better. We modeled the effect of policy interventions as shifting the payoff structure toward an assurance/stag-hunt game, where a coordinated, no-overplate equilibrium becomes achievable (though riskier off-equilibrium). We then built formal principal-agent utility models: the agent (student) maximizes hedonistic pleasure from food against guilt from waste; the principal (Bon App/the school) maximizes the effect of plate sizing and awareness campaigns against the cost of staff effort and time. We derived comparative statics via first-order conditions to see how the optimal amount of food on a tray responds to changes in student health-consciousness and food quality/aesthetics.
Post-policy, the game shifts from a single dominant-strategy equilibrium (mutual overplating) to two possible Nash equilibria, including a coordinated no-overplate outcome that is preferable for both players despite carrying more off-equilibrium risk. Comparative statics showed that increased health consciousness among students is predicted to reduce the total amount of food placed on a tray (a positive elasticity between health awareness and waste reduction). Drawing on cited literature, plate resizing alone has been shown to reduce food waste by about 19.5%, though our analysis suggests it is more effective in combination with other policies (communication, weight-based tracking, and awareness campaigns) than as a standalone intervention.
