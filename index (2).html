01 — Research Interests

My interest in this space grew directly out of my coursework and work experience — from modeling healthcare access disparities among Medicaid beneficiaries, to forecasting the drivers of rising U.S. healthcare spending with time series and VAR models. That work in a spatial statistics lab, estimating uncertainty in glacier volume loss, taught me that a number means little without a defensible account of how sure you are of it — a lesson that shapes how I approach health data now. What draws me to a PhD is using well-established quantitative methods in service of a specific public health question, rather than developing the methods themselves, to generate evidence that can inform real policy decisions at a state, national, or global level, on chronic disease and maternal & child health, including in low- and middle-income countries.

We investigate the volume loss of Storglaciären, an internationally studied glacier in northern Sweden, and address how to quantify uncertainty in that estimate when spatial correlation is present in the underlying elevation data. Using Digital Elevation Models (DEMs) derived from LiDAR and photogrammetry, we co-registered surface elevation surveys from different time points, reducing systematic bias (NMAD from 3.4m to 2.5m). We addressed data voids using McNabb's (2019) void-filling approach and hypsometric interpolation, binning elevation by 50m intervals and fitting a third-order polynomial model to predict missing values. This pipeline produces an elevation-difference map showing where the glacier has thinned most severely, laying the groundwork for a subsequent uncertainty estimate of total volume change that accounts for spatial correlation in the data — with the ultimate goal of writing this work up as an academic research paper.
In this research, we aim to explore the factors that influence Medicaid beneficiaries' access to healthcare. The data was sourced from the Applied Econometrics with R package. We utilized simple, multiple linear regression and logistic regression techniques to construct an analysis on the relationship between ethnicity and gender to access to health services. We employed stepwise, ridge and lasso regression to refine the model and identify other significant variables. Through our analysis we provide evidence that age, children, and first and second principal health components have some correlation with access to health services. Additionally, we uncovered a possible interaction effect between income and ethnicity, length of observation and ethnicity, and number of visits and ethnicity.

We modeled the level of conflict in refugee camps and level of effort (effectiveness) of conflict resolution strategies, addressing resource limitation, carrying capacity, and a nonlinear term to indicate larger conflicts require disproportionately more effort to resolve. We investigate the health, habitat use, and movement patterns of box turtles in Bay County, Florida, to understand ecological recovery following Hurricane Michael's 2018 habitat destruction. Using a multilevel regression model to account for repeated measurements on the same turtles over time, we analyzed 608 observations from 441 individually tagged turtles, examining how straight carapace length — a proxy for turtle health — relates to years since the hurricane, air temperature, and movement stationarity. We found that turtles captured later after the hurricane, at higher air temperatures, and while moving (rather than stationary) tended to have significantly larger carapace length, contrary to our initial expectation that stationary turtles would show better growth. These findings, while not causal, suggest environmental recovery and turtle mobility are linked, and point to further questions about how researchers can support both box turtle recovery and broader ecological recovery in the region.
Insights into the catalytic mechanism of yeast alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) by investigating its activity with various straight-chain primary alcohols. Focused on ethanol, methanol, 1-propanol, and 1-octanol, to compare binding affinity (Km) and enzyme turnover (Vmax). While previous research by Benner indicated that yeast ADH has a narrower substrate specificity and is most active with ethanol, we sought to expand the range of substrates and test how the increasing chain length of alcohols affects ADH reactivity. By employing the Michaelis-Menten model and conducting experiments with alcohol and NAD+ as substrates, we aimed to explore the reactivity patterns of yeast ADH.
We build and compare classification models to distinguish handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset, starting with a binary classification task (2s vs. 4s) and later extending to three classes (2s, 4s, and 6s). Using a simple engineered feature — the count of empty (non-inked) pixels per image — we fit and compared LDA and QDA models, finding QDA achieved higher accuracy (76.5% vs. 68%). To improve performance further, we engineered a second feature based on fitting a logistic regression to each image's ink-pixel coordinates and extracting its AIC value, then compared logistic regression and cross-validated KNN models on this feature. The logistic regression model achieved 75.5% accuracy on the two-digit task, but performance dropped sharply (to 56.5%) once a third digit (6) was introduced, with 6s frequently confused for 2s and 4s. We use confusion matrix analysis throughout to diagnose exactly where and why misclassification occurs as task complexity increases.
We built an interactive dashboard exploring the relationship between air quality, pollution-related death rates, population, and literacy across countries from 2010 to 2017, drawing on data from WHO, the World Bank, and Kaggle. Our research questions asked how air quality relates to death rates over time, how population size relates to air quality, and whether these relationships differ by region in ways that reflect a country's developmental status. We visualize global AQI levels geographically, break down death rates by pollution source (indoor, outdoor particulate, and ozone) across regions and years, and examine population trends alongside AQI. We find that death rate and population are associated with a country's air quality, and that literacy rate is negatively related to AQI — higher-literacy countries tend to have better air quality, though we don't establish this as a causal relationship.
Motivated by the argument that rising U.S. healthcare costs, not entitlement spending, pose the greatest threat to long-term fiscal stability, this paper investigates how personal consumption expenditure on tobacco, insurance, and housing and utilities relates to healthcare spending. Using annual U.S. data from 1959–2022 sourced from the Bureau of Economic Analysis, we test each series for stationarity using the Phillips-Perron test, then estimate a structural vector autoregression (VAR) model on the first-differenced, log-transformed variables. Impulse response functions show that a 1% shock to tobacco expenditure produces a significant decrease in healthcare expenditure, and a shock to housing and utilities expenditure is associated with a decrease in insurance expenditure. Forecast error variance decomposition shows tobacco shocks explain roughly 12% of healthcare expenditure variation after four years, with implications for policy interventions aimed at reducing tobacco consumption through healthcare-spending incentives.
We examine food waste at St. Olaf College's cafeteria as a principal-agent problem between the catering company (Bon App) and individual students, and model students' food-tray decisions using a utility framework balancing hedonistic pleasure against the guilt of wasted food. We frame the overplating decision as a prisoner's dilemma game between an individual student and the collective student body, showing that policy interventions — a weight-based waste-tracking threshold, plate resizing, and awareness campaigns — can shift the payoff structure toward an assurance/stag-hunt game with a preferable, coordinated equilibrium where students consistently avoid overplating. Using comparative statics, we show that increased health consciousness among students is predicted to reduce the total amount of food placed on a tray. We conclude that combining communication between catering staff and students, threshold-based measurement, and targeted awareness campaigns — rather than any single intervention alone — offers the most promising path to reducing food waste in college cafeterias.
